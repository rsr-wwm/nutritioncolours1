## Overview

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a water-soluble vitamin required for red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis, and nervous system function. It is unique among vitamins in requiring intrinsic factor — a protein made by stomach parietal cells — for efficient absorption in the small intestine.

## Forms

Cyanocobalamin (synthetic, stable, most common in supplements), methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin (naturally occurring active coenzyme forms), and hydroxocobalamin (commonly used in injections). All forms are converted to the active coenzyme forms in the body.

## Recommended intake

| Group | Amount per day |
| :--- | ---: |
| Adults 19+ | 2.4 mcg |
| Pregnancy | 2.6 mcg |
| Lactation | 2.8 mcg |

No Tolerable Upper Intake Level has been established; B12 is considered very safe even at high supplemental doses.

## Food sources

B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods: meat, fish, poultry, eggs, and dairy. Shellfish (especially clams) and organ meats are particularly rich sources. Vegans and vegetarians must rely on fortified foods (nutritional yeast, fortified plant milks and cereals) or supplements, since there are no reliable unfortified plant sources.

## Deficiency

Deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia and, if prolonged, can cause irreversible neurological damage — numbness, tingling, difficulty walking, and memory problems. Pernicious anemia, an autoimmune condition that destroys the cells producing intrinsic factor, is a common cause. Other risk groups include strict vegans, older adults with reduced stomach acid, people on long-term metformin or acid-reducing medications, and those who have had bariatric surgery.

## Too much

No toxicity has been established from oral B12 supplementation, even at high doses, because absorption is self-limiting and excess is excreted.
