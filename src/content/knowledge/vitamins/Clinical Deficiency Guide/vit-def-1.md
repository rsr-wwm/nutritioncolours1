## Overview

Vitamin deficiencies range from subtle and easily missed to severe, well-defined clinical syndromes. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are stored in body tissue, so deficiency develops gradually and is usually linked to fat malabsorption. Water-soluble vitamins (the B-complex and vitamin C) are not stored in large amounts, so deficiency can develop faster and is more directly tied to inadequate dietary intake.

## Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency signs

- **Vitamin A** — night blindness, dry eyes (xerophthalmia), impaired immune function
- **Vitamin D** — rickets in children; osteomalacia (bone pain, muscle weakness) in adults
- **Vitamin E** — peripheral nerve damage and muscle weakness (rare, usually from fat malabsorption)
- **Vitamin K** — easy bruising, bleeding, prolonged clotting time

## Water-soluble vitamin deficiency signs

- **B1 (thiamine)** — beriberi; Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, seen most often with alcohol use disorder
- **B2 (riboflavin)** — cracked lips, sore tongue, skin changes
- **B3 (niacin)** — pellagra: dermatitis, diarrhea, and cognitive changes
- **B6 (pyridoxine)** — anemia, dermatitis, peripheral neuropathy
- **B9 (folate)** — megaloblastic anemia; neural tube defects if deficient during early pregnancy
- **B12 (cobalamin)** — megaloblastic anemia and neurological symptoms
- **C (ascorbic acid)** — scurvy: bleeding gums, poor wound healing, fatigue

## Who is at risk

People with malabsorption conditions (celiac disease, Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis, prior bariatric surgery), those on restrictive diets, older adults, pregnant and breastfeeding women, people with alcohol use disorder, and those taking medications that interfere with nutrient absorption (proton pump inhibitors, metformin, certain anticonvulsants) are at elevated risk for one or more vitamin deficiencies.

## Laboratory diagnosis

Diagnosis typically relies on serum or plasma levels of the specific vitamin, sometimes combined with a functional marker — for example, methylmalonic acid for B12 status, or prothrombin time/INR for vitamin K status. Test results should be interpreted by a clinician alongside symptoms and risk factors, since reference ranges and functional significance vary by nutrient.
