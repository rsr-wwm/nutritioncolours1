## Overview

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin and antioxidant essential for collagen synthesis, wound healing, immune function, and iron absorption. Humans cannot synthesize vitamin C and must obtain it from diet.

## Forms

Naturally occurring L-ascorbic acid and synthetic ascorbic acid are chemically identical. Buffered forms (sodium ascorbate, calcium ascorbate) and liposomal formulations exist mainly to reduce GI irritation or improve absorption, but plain ascorbic acid is well absorbed for most people.

## Recommended intake

| Group | Amount per day |
| :--- | ---: |
| Men 19+ | 90 mg |
| Women 19+ | 75 mg |
| Smokers | +35 mg |
| Pregnancy | 85 mg |
| Lactation | 120 mg |

**Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL):** 2,000 mg/day for adults, based on the risk of gastrointestinal upset.

## Food sources

Concentrated in fruits and vegetables: citrus fruits, red and green peppers, kiwifruit, strawberries, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and tomatoes. Guava, blackcurrants, and rose hips are especially rich sources.

## Functions

Collagen synthesis (hydroxylation of proline and lysine), antioxidant activity that helps regenerate vitamin E, enhancement of non-heme iron absorption, and support of immune cell function.

## Deficiency — scurvy

Severe, prolonged deficiency causes scurvy: fatigue, gum disease and bleeding gums, poor wound healing, and joint pain. It is rare today but can still occur in people with very restricted diets, alcohol use disorder, or malabsorption conditions.

## Too much

Vitamin C is generally very safe, and excess is excreted in urine. High doses can cause diarrhea, nausea, and cramps. People prone to oxalate kidney stones or with hereditary hemochromatosis (which increases iron absorption further with high vitamin C intake) should be more cautious with high-dose supplementation.
