## Overview

Vitamin D is unusual among vitamins because the body can synthesize it in the skin from sunlight exposure. Food and supplement sources matter most for people with limited sun exposure, darker skin, higher latitudes, or during winter months.

## Forms

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) comes from animal sources and is generally more effective at raising blood vitamin D levels. Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) comes from plant and fungal sources, including UV-exposed mushrooms, and is somewhat less potent. Both forms are converted by the liver and kidneys into the active hormone form.

## Food sources

Fatty fish — salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring — are the best natural food sources. Egg yolks contain modest amounts. UV-exposed mushrooms contain vitamin D2. Many countries fortify milk, plant milks, orange juice, and cereals with vitamin D, and these fortified foods are a major source of intake for many people.

## Recommended intake

| Group | Amount per day |
| :--- | ---: |
| Ages 1–70 | 600 IU (15 mcg) |
| Ages 70+ | 800 IU (20 mcg) |

**Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL):** 4,000 IU/day for adults.

## Deficiency

Vitamin D deficiency is common, particularly in winter, at higher latitudes, in people with darker skin, and in those with limited sun exposure. Deficiency in children causes rickets; in adults it causes osteomalacia — bone pain and muscle weakness. Blood testing for 25-hydroxyvitamin D is used to assess status.

## Too much

Toxicity from food or sun exposure does not occur. Sustained high-dose supplementation can cause hypercalcemia (nausea, weakness, kidney problems), but this is uncommon at typical supplemental doses and usually requires doses well above the UL over a prolonged period.
