## Overview

Choline is an essential nutrient the body can partly synthesize but generally not in sufficient amounts, so dietary intake is needed. It is a precursor for acetylcholine (a neurotransmitter), phosphatidylcholine (a key component of cell membranes), and betaine (a methyl donor). Choline has an Adequate Intake (AI) rather than a full RDA, because the data needed to set a formal recommendation are still limited.

## Recommended intake (Adequate Intake)

| Group | Amount per day |
| :--- | ---: |
| Men 19+ | 550 mg |
| Women 19+ | 425 mg |
| Pregnancy | 450 mg |
| Lactation | 550 mg |

**Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL):** 3,500 mg/day for adults, based on the risk of low blood pressure and a fishy body odor.

## Food sources

Eggs (especially the yolk), meat, fish, dairy, and cruciferous vegetables such as broccoli are good sources. Soy products also contribute choline. Plant-based diets tend to be lower in choline, and many prenatal vitamins are inadequate in choline — a concern given its role in fetal brain development.

## Forms and supplements

Common supplemental forms include choline bitartrate and choline citrate. Alpha-GPC and CDP-choline (citicoline) are more concentrated forms studied for cognitive support, though evidence for routine use in healthy people is limited. Betaine (trimethylglycine) is a related methyl donor but is chemically and functionally distinct from choline itself.

## Deficiency

Overt deficiency is uncommon but can cause liver damage (fatty liver) and muscle damage; it is most often seen with prolonged inadequate intake or in the context of specific metabolic conditions.

## Too much

High supplemental doses can cause a fishy body odor, low blood pressure, and gastrointestinal upset.
