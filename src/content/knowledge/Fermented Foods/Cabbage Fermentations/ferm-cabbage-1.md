## Overview

Sauerkraut is finely shredded cabbage fermented in its own brine by lactic acid bacteria naturally present on the leaves — no starter culture or vinegar is used in the traditional method. Salt (roughly 1.5–2% of the cabbage's weight) draws out water to form the brine, creates an anaerobic environment, and inhibits spoilage organisms while lactic acid bacteria (starting with *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, then dominated by *Lactobacillus plantarum*) acidify the mixture to a pH of around 3.5, which is what preserves it.

## Nutritional changes from fermentation

Fermentation increases the bioavailability of some nutrients: vitamin C content is largely retained (raw cabbage is already a decent source), B-vitamin content increases modestly, and the process makes iron somewhat more bioavailable. Raw, unpasteurized sauerkraut retains live lactic acid bacteria; pasteurized or shelf-stable canned versions do not, and vinegar-pickled cabbage that hasn't been fermented contains no live cultures at all — checking the label (refrigerated section, "unpasteurized," or "raw") is the only reliable way to know which type you're buying.

## Evidence on health effects

Fermented vegetables including sauerkraut are a source of probiotic lactic acid bacteria, and observational research links regular fermented-food intake with markers of gut microbiome diversity. Human clinical trial evidence specifically for sauerkraut (as opposed to fermented foods broadly) remains limited; much of the strongest probiotic evidence in this food category comes from studies of yogurt and kimchi rather than sauerkraut itself.

## Safety notes

- Homemade sauerkraut should be checked daily during fermentation: white, wrinkled kahm yeast on the surface is generally harmless (skim it off), but fuzzy, pink, or black mold means the batch should be discarded.
- Store-bought sauerkraut, like other fermented vegetables, is relatively high in sodium — a consideration for people managing blood pressure.
