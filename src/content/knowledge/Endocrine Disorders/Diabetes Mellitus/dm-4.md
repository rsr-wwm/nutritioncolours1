## Overview

Hypoglycemia (low blood glucose) and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA, a buildup of ketones and acid in the blood from insulin deficiency) are acute complications of diabetes that can become medical emergencies. Nutrition has a defined, limited role in each: treating mild-to-moderate hypoglycemia in the moment, and supporting day-to-day prevention — but neither DKA nor severe hypoglycemia is managed through diet alone.

## Nutrition considerations

- **The ADA's "Rule of 15"**: For conscious individuals with mild-to-moderate hypoglycemia, the widely taught approach is to consume about 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate (such as glucose tablets or juice), wait 15 minutes, and recheck blood glucose, repeating if still low. This is a well-established self-treatment protocol, not a general dietary strategy.
- **Preventive eating patterns**: Consistent meal timing, not skipping meals when on insulin or sulfonylureas, and carrying fast-acting carbohydrate for situations like exercise are standard prevention practices.
- **Sick-day management**: During illness, the NIDDK and ADA recommend continued fluid intake and monitoring, since illness can raise glucose and ketone risk even with reduced appetite — insulin should generally not be stopped during illness without specific clinician instructions.
- **Severe hypoglycemia**: If someone is unconscious or unable to swallow safely, glucagon (an injectable or nasal medication) is the appropriate treatment — not food or drink, which pose a choking risk.

## Safety note

Severe hypoglycemia and DKA are medical emergencies requiring immediate treatment — DKA in particular requires hospital-level care with fluids, electrolyte management, and IV insulin. This page is educational only; anyone experiencing symptoms of either condition should seek emergency care immediately rather than attempting dietary self-treatment.
