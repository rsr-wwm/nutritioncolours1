## Overview

Soluble fiber dissolves in water to form a viscous gel. This physical property is the basis for most of its studied health effects: it binds bile acids (which the liver then has to replace using cholesterol, lowering blood LDL), slows gastric emptying (blunting post-meal blood sugar spikes), and is fermented by gut bacteria into short-chain fatty acids. Most clinical benefit is seen with roughly 5–10 g of soluble fiber per day, on top of overall dietary fiber.

## Key sources and evidence

- **Psyllium husk** (*Plantago ovata* seed husk, the basis of products like Metamucil) is one of the most concentrated soluble fiber sources available (roughly 70–85% soluble fiber by weight). Clinical trials support cholesterol-lowering effects at doses around 6–12 g/day, and it is well-studied for both constipation and diarrhea-predominant bowel irregularity. It must be taken with adequate water — taking it dry or with too little fluid has caused rare cases of esophageal or bowel obstruction.
- **Oat beta-glucan** (a 1,3;1,4-beta-D-glucan found in oats and barley) has an FDA-authorized health claim: about 3 g/day is associated with reduced LDL cholesterol as part of a diet low in saturated fat. A cup of cooked oatmeal provides roughly 2–3 g; oat bran provides more per serving than rolled oats.
- **Chia seed** (*Salvia hispanica*) is around 40% fiber by weight, most of it soluble, and forms a thick mucilage gel that can hold roughly 10 times its weight in water when soaked. It also contributes plant-based omega-3 (ALA). Soaking chia for about 10 minutes before eating (as in chia pudding) improves comfort and digestibility versus eating it dry.
- **Legumes** (lentils, black beans, chickpeas, split peas) combine soluble fiber with resistant starch, especially when cooked and cooled, and are associated with favorable long-term health outcomes in cohort studies, likely due to their overall fiber and nutrient package rather than any single compound.

## Practical targets

USDA dietary guidelines target roughly 25 g/day of total fiber for adult women and 38 g/day for adult men; most American adults consume well below this, often around 15 g/day. Whole-food sources are generally preferred over supplements as a first approach, since they also deliver other nutrients, though supplements like psyllium have the strongest individual clinical trial evidence for cholesterol reduction specifically.

## Safety and contraindications

- Increase fiber gradually with adequate water intake to minimize gas, bloating, and cramping.
- Psyllium and other bulk-forming fibers can reduce absorption of some oral medications if taken at the same time — a general rule of thumb is to separate fiber supplements from medications by 1–2 hours; check with a pharmacist for specific drugs.
- People with a history of bowel strictures or swallowing difficulty should use bulk fiber supplements only under medical guidance.
