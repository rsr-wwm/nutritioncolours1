## Overview

Gallstones are hardened deposits (usually cholesterol, sometimes pigment stones) that form in the gallbladder. Most gallstones cause no symptoms and are found incidentally, but some cause biliary colic (episodic upper-abdominal pain, often after fatty meals) or, if a stone blocks a duct, more serious complications like cholecystitis (gallbladder inflammation) or cholangitis (bile duct infection), which are medical emergencies.

## Risk factors and diet

Well-established risk factors include obesity, rapid weight loss (including after bariatric surgery), pregnancy, female sex, age over 40, and a diet high in refined carbohydrates and saturated fat. Conversely, diets higher in fiber, healthy fats (like those in nuts and olive oil), and moderate, steady weight management are associated with lower gallstone risk in observational research — very rapid or crash weight loss actually increases risk, so gradual weight loss is preferred if weight loss is a goal.

## Safety — when it's an emergency

Seek immediate medical care for severe or persistent upper-abdominal pain, especially with fever, jaundice (yellowing skin/eyes), or vomiting — these can indicate cholecystitis or cholangitis, which need urgent treatment and often surgery (laparoscopic cholecystectomy) or an ERCP procedure to clear a blocked duct.
