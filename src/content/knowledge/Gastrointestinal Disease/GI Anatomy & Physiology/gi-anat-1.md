## Overview

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract is a continuous tube running from the mouth to the anus — esophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), and large intestine (colon and rectum) — supported by accessory organs (liver, gallbladder, and pancreas) that supply bile and digestive enzymes. Food moves through this tract via coordinated muscle contractions and sphincters, being progressively broken down and absorbed along the way.

## Why this matters for nutrition

- **Stomach**: acid and enzymes begin breaking down protein and release intrinsic factor, which is needed later for vitamin B12 absorption.
- **Small intestine**: the primary site of nutrient absorption — the duodenum absorbs iron, calcium, and folate; the jejunum absorbs most carbohydrates, protein, and fats; the ileum absorbs vitamin B12 and bile acids.
- **Large intestine (colon)**: absorbs water and electrolytes and hosts the gut microbiome, which ferments fiber into short-chain fatty acids.
- **Liver, gallbladder, pancreas**: the liver makes bile (stored in the gallbladder) to help digest fats, and the pancreas supplies enzymes for carbohydrates, fats, and proteins plus bicarbonate to neutralize stomach acid.

Because different nutrients are absorbed at different points along this tract, conditions or surgeries affecting a specific segment (for example, ileal disease affecting B12 absorption, or gastric surgery affecting iron absorption) tend to cause predictable, segment-specific nutrient gaps — which is why clinicians target nutrient testing to the anatomy involved.

## Safety

This is general anatomical information, not medical advice. If you have a diagnosed GI condition or have had GI surgery, ask your doctor or a registered dietitian which specific nutrients you should have monitored.
