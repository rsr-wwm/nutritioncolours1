## Overview

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are the two main forms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) — chronic, immune-mediated inflammation of the digestive tract. Crohn's disease can affect any part of the GI tract from the mouth to the anus, often in a patchy pattern with full-thickness bowel wall inflammation that can lead to strictures or fistulas. Ulcerative colitis is confined to the colon and rectum, with continuous, superficial inflammation of the inner lining. Diagnosis combines symptom history, blood and stool markers (such as fecal calprotectin), endoscopy, biopsy, and imaging.

## Nutritional & Dietary Considerations

- No single diet cures or reliably induces IBD remission, but nutrition plays a genuine supportive role. Exclusive enteral nutrition is an established option to induce remission in Crohn's disease, particularly in children and adolescents.
- During active flares, a low-fiber, low-residue diet may temporarily ease symptoms — especially useful if strictures are present. During remission, most patients are encouraged to eat as varied and balanced a diet as they can tolerate.
- Nutrient deficiencies are common in IBD and should be screened for, especially iron, vitamin B12 (particularly with Crohn's disease affecting the ileum), vitamin D, calcium, and folate.
- Smaller, more frequent meals and steady hydration can ease symptoms related to malabsorption or active inflammation.

## Safety Note

Nutrition strategies support, but do not replace, medical therapy for IBD, which may include immunomodulators, biologics, or surgery. Work with a gastroenterologist and, where possible, a registered dietitian — especially during flares or when strictures are present.

The pathophysiology of ibd 1 is deeply interconnected with systemic metabolic pathways. When analyzing the cellular microenvironment, it is evident that targeted nutrient availability plays a crucial role in mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades. Deficiencies in these key areas often exacerbate the underlying mechanisms of ibd 1, leading to an increased frequency of acute exacerbations and a general decline in the patient's quality of life.
