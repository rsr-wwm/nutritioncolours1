## Overview

For people who've had a kidney stone, especially recurrent stones, the NIDDK and NKF recommend a metabolic workup — often a 24-hour urine collection — to identify what's actually driving stone formation (low urine volume, high calcium or oxalate excretion, low citrate, high uric acid, or abnormal urine pH). This turns prevention from generic advice into a plan targeted at the specific abnormality found.

## Nutritional considerations

- **Fluids**: The single best-supported prevention measure across stone types is drinking enough fluid to produce roughly 2–2.5 liters of urine per day, spread through the day rather than all at once.
- **Sodium**: Reducing sodium intake lowers urinary calcium excretion, which is relevant to preventing calcium-based stones (the most common type).
- **Dietary calcium**: Counterintuitively, NIDDK guidance does not recommend restricting dietary calcium for calcium oxalate stone formers — adequate dietary calcium eaten with meals actually binds oxalate in the gut and can reduce the amount absorbed and excreted in urine. Restricting calcium too far can increase oxalate absorption and, separately, harm bone health.
- **Animal protein**: High intake of animal protein can increase urinary calcium and uric acid while lowering citrate (a natural stone inhibitor), so moderating it is a common general recommendation, particularly for recurrent stone formers.
- **Citrate**: Citrus fruits (like lemons) are sometimes encouraged since citrate helps inhibit stone crystal formation, though this is a supportive measure rather than a standalone treatment.

## Safety note

The details of an effective prevention diet depend on the specific metabolic abnormality identified in a stone analysis or 24-hour urine test — a generic "kidney stone diet" applied without this workup may target the wrong risk factor. Prevention plans should be built with a urologist, nephrologist, or renal dietitian based on individual results.
