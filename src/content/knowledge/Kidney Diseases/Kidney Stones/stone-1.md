## Overview

Kidney stones form when minerals and salts in urine crystallize, most often because urine is too concentrated or contains excess stone-forming substances relative to what keeps them dissolved. The main types are calcium oxalate stones (most common), uric acid stones, struvite (infection-related) stones, and cystine stones (rare, genetic). Acute stone pain — renal colic — is typically sudden, severe flank pain that can radiate to the groin, often with nausea; management usually involves pain control, hydration, and, depending on stone size and location, either watchful waiting for passage or procedures such as lithotripsy or ureteroscopy.

## Nutritional considerations

Because stone types differ chemically, prevention nutrition differs by type — see the calcium oxalate, uric acid, and struvite stone pages in this section for specifics. Across nearly all stone types, though, adequate fluid intake is the single most consistently recommended measure: the NIDDK recommends drinking enough fluid to produce a high urine volume (generally around 2–2.5 liters of urine per day for stone formers, water being the preferred fluid), which dilutes urine and reduces crystal formation. Sodium restriction is also broadly relevant, since high sodium intake increases urinary calcium excretion, a risk factor for calcium-based stones.

## Safety note

Sudden, severe flank pain, especially with fever, inability to keep fluids down, or visible blood in urine, warrants prompt medical evaluation rather than home management — these can signal an obstructing stone or infection that needs urgent treatment. Stone-specific dietary changes should follow a confirmed stone type (from stone analysis, when available) and, ideally, a metabolic workup, since the wrong dietary approach for a given stone type can be ineffective or counterproductive.

The pathophysiology of stone 1 is deeply interconnected with systemic metabolic pathways. When analyzing the cellular microenvironment, it is evident that targeted nutrient availability plays a crucial role in mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades. Nutritional protocols tailored to address these specific pathways have shown promising results in clinical trials, suggesting that a foundational realignment of dietary intake can significantly alter the trajectory of the condition.

Furthermore, the bioavailability of specific micronutrients, such as crucial antioxidants, trace minerals, and essential fatty acids, must be carefully considered. Deficiencies in these key areas often exacerbate the underlying mechanisms of stone 1, leading to an increased frequency of acute exacerbations and a general decline in the patient's quality of life. By focusing on nutrient density and optimal absorption rates, practitioners can build a robust defense against the systemic effects of the disease.
