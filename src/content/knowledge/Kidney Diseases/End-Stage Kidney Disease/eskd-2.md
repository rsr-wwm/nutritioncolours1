## Overview

Hemodialysis filters blood outside the body through a machine, typically three times a week for about 3–4 hours per session at a center (or more frequently for some home hemodialysis regimens). It requires vascular access — commonly an arteriovenous fistula or graft, or a central venous catheter for shorter-term use. Because filtration happens intermittently rather than continuously, waste products, fluid, and electrolytes build up between sessions, which shapes the nutrition approach.

## Nutritional considerations

Hemodialysis nutrition is built around the gaps between sessions:

- **Protein**: Needs are generally higher than in pre-dialysis CKD, since hemodialysis removes amino acids along with toxins; the NKF notes hemodialysis patients typically require more protein than the general population, with the exact target set by a renal dietitian.
- **Fluid**: Because urine output is often reduced or absent, fluid intake is usually restricted between sessions to limit interdialytic weight gain and reduce strain on the heart during dialysis — the individual allowance depends on residual urine output and is set by the care team.
- **Sodium**: Kept moderate to limit thirst and fluid retention between sessions, supporting the fluid restriction above.
- **Potassium and phosphorus**: Typically restricted, since intermittent dialysis clears them less continuously than healthy kidneys would; phosphate binders are commonly prescribed alongside dietary limits.

## Safety note

Hemodialysis nutrition targets (protein, fluid allowance, potassium, phosphorus) are set individually based on residual kidney function, dialysis adequacy, and lab trends, and are reviewed regularly by the dialysis unit's renal dietitian — they should not be estimated from general figures.
