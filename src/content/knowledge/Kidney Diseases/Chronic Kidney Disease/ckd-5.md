## Overview

As CKD progresses, three complication clusters become more common. Anemia develops because damaged kidneys produce less erythropoietin, the hormone that signals bone marrow to make red blood cells. CKD-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) arises from disrupted calcium, phosphorus, vitamin D, and parathyroid hormone balance as kidney function declines. Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in CKD patients, driven by shared risk factors (hypertension, diabetes) plus CKD-specific contributors like vascular calcification and chronic inflammation.

## Nutritional considerations

- **Anemia**: Iron status (both dietary and, when prescribed, supplemental iron), along with adequate B12 and folate, supports red blood cell production, though moderate-to-severe CKD-related anemia is primarily managed medically (erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, IV iron) rather than through diet alone.
- **Bone disease (CKD-MBD)**: Calcium, phosphorus, and vitamin D intake are balanced individually based on lab values (calcium, phosphorus, PTH, vitamin D) rather than a blanket rule; phosphorus from food additives is often limited first, since it's absorbed more readily than naturally occurring phosphorus. Calcium and vitamin D supplementation should only be adjusted under medical guidance, since both too little and too much can worsen bone and vascular outcomes in CKD.
- **Cardiovascular risk**: A heart-healthy pattern — limiting sodium, limiting saturated fat, favoring vegetables, fruit, and whole grains within whatever potassium/phosphorus limits apply — supports blood pressure and lipid management alongside prescribed medications.

## Safety note

Anemia and bone-mineral labs in CKD (hemoglobin, iron studies, calcium, phosphorus, PTH, vitamin D) should guide any supplementation — taking iron, calcium, or vitamin D supplements without testing can mask problems or push levels to unsafe extremes in someone with reduced kidney clearance.
