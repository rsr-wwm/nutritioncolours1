## Overview

Routine cancer screening aims to detect cancer early, often before symptoms appear, when treatment is generally more effective. Common evidence-based screening tests include mammography for breast cancer, colonoscopy (or other stool- or scope-based tests) for colorectal cancer, Pap smear and/or HPV testing for cervical cancer, and low-dose CT scanning for lung cancer in adults at high risk based on smoking history. Screening schedules and starting ages are individualized based on personal and family risk factors, so patients should discuss the right plan with their physician.

## Nutrition-Related Considerations Around Screening

- **Colonoscopy preparation**: Most colonoscopy prep protocols require a clear liquid or low-residue diet for a day or more before the procedure, along with a bowel-cleansing solution, to allow clear visualization of the colon. Specific instructions should always follow the prescribing physician's protocol.
- **General health before procedures**: Adequate hydration and following pre-procedure fasting instructions (for tests requiring sedation) support safety and comfort.

## Diet and Cancer Risk

For colorectal cancer specifically, the WCRF/AICR Continuous Update Project identifies strong evidence that eating less red and processed meat, and more whole grains and fiber-containing foods, is associated with lower risk. More broadly, maintaining a healthy body weight, staying physically active, and limiting alcohol are associated with lower risk of several cancers detectable through screening, including breast and colorectal cancer. These general health measures complement, but do not replace, recommended screening tests.

> **Important:** This information does not replace individualized screening recommendations from your physician. Screening schedules should be based on your personal risk factors, age, and family history — always follow the guidance of your treating physician.
