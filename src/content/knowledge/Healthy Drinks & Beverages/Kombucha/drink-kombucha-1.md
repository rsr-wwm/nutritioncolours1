## Overview

Kombucha is sweetened tea fermented by a SCOBY (symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast), which converts sugar and tea into a tangy, lightly effervescent drink containing organic acids (notably acetic acid), trace alcohol, B vitamins, and live microbes. It has roots in East Asian tradition and has become widely commercialized in the U.S. and Europe over the past two decades.

## Active compounds and evidence

Kombucha contains live bacteria and yeast, similar in concept to other fermented foods, and is often marketed for gut health via its probiotic content. The human clinical evidence specifically for kombucha, however, is limited — most gut-microbiome and health claims are extrapolated from broader fermented-food research or from animal studies, not large human trials of kombucha itself. It is reasonable to view kombucha as a plausible contributor to a varied, fermented-food-inclusive diet, without treating it as a proven therapeutic beverage.

## Safety and contraindications

- **Trace alcohol:** Fermentation produces small amounts of alcohol, generally under 0.5% ABV in commercial products but sometimes higher in homemade batches; this matters for people avoiding alcohol entirely (e.g., during pregnancy, in recovery, or for religious/personal reasons).
- **Contamination risk in homemade batches:** The FDA and CDC have documented cases of illness linked to improperly fermented homemade kombucha, including contamination with harmful bacteria or mold, and cases of excess acidity causing acidosis in immunocompromised individuals. Store-bought, commercially produced kombucha carries much lower risk than homemade batches from an unverified culture.
- **Immunocompromised individuals:** Because kombucha contains live microbes, the CDC and other health authorities generally advise people who are pregnant, immunocompromised, or have compromised gut lining conditions to consult a clinician before regular consumption.
- **Sugar content:** Some bottled kombuchas contain substantial added or residual sugar; checking labels matters for those managing blood sugar.

## How it's typically prepared

Sweetened black or green tea is fermented with a SCOBY for roughly 7–14 days at room temperature, then often bottled with fruit or flavorings for a second, carbonation-producing fermentation.
