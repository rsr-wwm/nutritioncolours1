## Overview

Hibiscus tea is a tart, deep-red infusion made from the dried calyces (the sepal structure, not the petals) of *Hibiscus sabdariffa* (roselle). It is known regionally as karkade (Egypt/Sudan), agua de Jamaica (Mexico), sorrel (Caribbean), bissap (West Africa), and zobo (Nigeria). It is caffeine-free and rich in anthocyanins (the pigments giving it its crimson color), along with vitamin C and organic acids.

## Evidence on blood pressure

Hibiscus tea is one of the better-studied herbal teas for cardiovascular effects. A randomized controlled trial published in the *Journal of Nutrition* (McKay et al., 2010) found that drinking three cups of hibiscus tea daily for six weeks modestly lowered systolic blood pressure in adults with prehypertension or mild hypertension, compared with a placebo beverage. Other smaller trials have reported similar directional effects. The proposed mechanisms include mild ACE-inhibitor-like activity and a diuretic effect. This is a real but modest effect — hibiscus tea is not a substitute for prescribed antihypertensive medication, and anyone on blood pressure medication should discuss regular hibiscus consumption with their clinician to avoid additive hypotension.

## Safety and contraindications

- **Blood pressure medication:** Because of its own blood-pressure-lowering effect, combining regular hibiscus tea with antihypertensive drugs warrants medical guidance.
- **Pregnancy:** Hibiscus has traditionally been used to induce menstruation/labor in some cultures; pregnant women are generally advised to avoid regular consumption pending more safety data.
- **Iron absorption:** Like other polyphenol-rich teas, hibiscus may modestly reduce non-heme iron absorption if taken with iron-rich meals — drinking it between meals avoids this.

## How it's typically prepared

1–2 teaspoons of dried calyces (or ¼ cup fresh) steeped in just-boiled water for 5–10 minutes; commonly served iced with a little honey or sugar to balance the tartness.
