## Overview

Chamomile tea is an infusion made from the dried flower heads of German chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*, also called *M. recutita*) or, less commonly, Roman chamomile (*Chamaemelum nobile*). It is caffeine-free and one of the most widely used traditional teas for relaxation and mild digestive discomfort.

## Active compounds and evidence

Chamomile flowers contain the flavonoid **apigenin**, along with bisabolol and chamazulene. Apigenin binds to benzodiazepine receptors in the brain, which is the proposed mechanism behind chamomile's mild sedative and anti-anxiety effects. Small clinical trials support a modest benefit for sleep quality and generalized anxiety symptoms, though the evidence base is limited in size and quality — NCCIH describes it as promising but not conclusive.

Traditionally it is also used for mild indigestion and as a soothing after-meal drink, though rigorous evidence for digestive benefits specifically from the tea (as opposed to concentrated extracts) is thin.

## Safety and contraindications

- **Allergy risk:** Chamomile belongs to the Asteraceae/Compositae family (ragweed, daisies, marigolds). People with allergies to these plants can react to chamomile.
- **Blood thinners:** Chamomile contains coumarin-like compounds and may theoretically increase bleeding risk when combined with anticoagulant medication (e.g., warfarin) — caution advised, especially before surgery.
- **Pregnancy:** Traditional use raises concerns about uterine stimulant effects at high doses; food-amount tea is generally considered low risk, but pregnant patients should check with their clinician.

## How it's typically prepared

1–2 teaspoons of dried flowers steeped in hot (not boiling) water for 5–10 minutes, covered to retain volatile oils.
