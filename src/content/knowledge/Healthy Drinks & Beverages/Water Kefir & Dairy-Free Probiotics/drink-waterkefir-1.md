## Overview

Water kefir (also called tibicos) is a fermented, lightly carbonated beverage made by culturing sugar water with water kefir "grains" — a different symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast than milk kefir grains, adapted to a sugar-water rather than dairy environment. It's a common dairy-free alternative for people who want a kefir-style fermented drink without milk, and is often flavored with fruit juice or ginger during a second fermentation.

## Active compounds and evidence

Like other fermented beverages, water kefir contains live bacteria and yeast, though its specific microbial profile differs from both milk kefir and kombucha. Direct human clinical research on water kefir is limited; most of what's known comes from microbiology and food-science characterization of the culture itself rather than clinical trials on health outcomes. It's reasonable to view it as a low-calorie, dairy-free, fermented beverage option rather than one with established specific health claims.

## Safety and contraindications

- **Sugar content:** Water kefir starts as sugar water; most of the sugar is consumed during fermentation, but residual sugar content varies by ferment time and recipe — those managing blood sugar should be mindful, especially with flavored/bottled versions.
- **Trace alcohol:** Fermentation produces a small amount of alcohol, generally low but higher with longer ferments or a second fermentation; relevant for those avoiding alcohol entirely.
- **Homemade fermentation risk:** As with kombucha, improperly maintained cultures can allow contamination; sourcing grains from a reputable supplier and following food-safety practices (clean equipment, appropriate fermentation vessels) reduces this risk.
- **Immunocompromised individuals:** Discuss regular consumption of any live-culture fermented beverage with a clinician if significantly immunocompromised.

## How it's typically prepared

Water kefir grains are added to sugar water (sometimes with added minerals like dried fruit for micronutrients the grains need) and fermented at room temperature for roughly 24–48 hours, then strained and often bottled with fruit juice for a flavored, carbonated second ferment.
