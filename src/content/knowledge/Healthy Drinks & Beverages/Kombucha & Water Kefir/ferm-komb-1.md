## Overview

Kombucha begins as sweetened tea (typically black or green) fermented by a SCOBY — a symbiotic culture of acetic-acid bacteria and yeast that forms a rubbery "mother" on the surface. Over roughly one to two weeks, the culture consumes sugar and produces organic acids (mainly acetic acid, giving kombucha its vinegar-like tang), carbon dioxide (natural carbonation), trace alcohol, and a population of live microbes carried over into the finished drink.

## Active compounds and evidence

Because kombucha is a fermented, tea-based drink, it inherits some of tea's polyphenol content along with organic acids and live microbes from fermentation. Marketing often extends this into broad "detox" and gut-health claims; the direct human evidence for kombucha specifically remains sparse, consisting mostly of small studies, case reports, and extrapolation from general fermented-food and probiotic research rather than large randomized trials of kombucha itself. It's reasonable to treat kombucha as one of several fermented foods that may support dietary variety, without over-claiming specific clinical outcomes.

## Safety and contraindications

- **Trace alcohol:** Commercial kombucha in the U.S. is generally kept under 0.5% ABV to be sold as a non-alcoholic beverage, but homemade or "hard" kombucha can contain meaningfully more; check labels if avoiding alcohol.
- **Homemade fermentation risk:** Improperly maintained cultures or non-sterile equipment can allow harmful bacteria or mold to grow; the FDA and CDC have documented illness linked to home-brewed kombucha.
- **Excess acidity:** Case reports exist of acidosis and other adverse effects in immunocompromised individuals consuming large quantities of kombucha; moderate intake and commercially produced product are the safer default.
- **Pregnancy:** Because of trace alcohol and live-culture content, many clinicians advise caution or avoidance during pregnancy.

## How it's typically prepared

Sweetened tea is fermented at room temperature with an active SCOBY for about 7–14 days (first fermentation), often followed by a bottled second fermentation with fruit to build carbonation and flavor.
