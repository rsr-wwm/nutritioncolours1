## Overview

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a blood clot that forms in a deep vein, most often in the leg. If part of the clot breaks off and travels to the lungs, it causes a pulmonary embolism (PE) — a potentially life-threatening blockage of blood flow. Together these are referred to as venous thromboembolism (VTE). Risk factors follow the classic framework of Virchow's triad: blood stasis (from immobility or long travel), vessel injury (from surgery or trauma), and a tendency toward excess clotting (from certain cancers, genetic conditions, or hormonal factors). Diagnosis typically combines clinical risk scoring (such as the Wells score), a D-dimer blood test, and imaging (ultrasound for DVT, CT pulmonary angiography for PE). Treatment centers on anticoagulation ("blood thinners") — options include heparin-based injectables, warfarin, or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) — with the specific choice and duration determined by the treating physician based on the cause and risk profile.

## Nutritional Considerations

The most clinically important nutrition interaction in VTE treatment involves **warfarin**, one of the anticoagulants used for DVT/PE. Warfarin works by interfering with vitamin K–dependent clotting factors, which means dietary vitamin K intake directly affects how well warfarin controls blood clotting. This is a well-established, clinically significant interaction: the general medical guidance is not to avoid vitamin K–rich foods (such as leafy greens like spinach, kale, and broccoli) but rather to keep vitamin K intake **consistent** from week to week, since sudden large changes — either increases or decreases — can throw off warfarin's effect and the international normalized ratio (INR) blood test used to monitor it. Patients on warfarin typically work with their prescribing physician or an anticoagulation clinic to establish this consistent intake pattern, and INR is monitored regularly to keep dosing accurate. Newer anticoagulants (DOACs) generally do not have this same vitamin K interaction, though they carry their own considerations set by the prescribing physician. Beyond the warfarin-vitamin K interaction, general nutrition support (adequate hydration, maintaining a healthy weight, and, where relevant, weight-conscious eating to reduce venous pressure) can be a reasonable supportive measure, though it does not replace anticoagulation therapy.

## Safety

VTE, particularly pulmonary embolism, can be life-threatening and requires prompt medical diagnosis and treatment. Anyone on warfarin should never make major changes to their vitamin K intake without informing their prescribing clinician, since this can dangerously alter clotting risk in either direction. This content does not provide specific vitamin K gram targets or INR thresholds — those must be individualized by the prescribing physician or anticoagulation clinic.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1: Should someone on warfarin avoid leafy greens and other vitamin K–rich foods?
**A1:** Generally no — the standard guidance is to eat a consistent amount of vitamin K–rich foods week to week rather than avoiding them, since sudden changes (increases or decreases) are what disrupt warfarin's effect. Any dietary changes should be discussed with the prescribing physician or anticoagulation clinic.

### Q2: Do all blood thinners interact with vitamin K?
**A2:** No — this interaction is specific to warfarin. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) generally do not have the same vitamin K interaction, though patients should always confirm any food or drug interactions with their prescribing physician.

### Q3: What are warning signs of a pulmonary embolism that need emergency care?
**A3:** Sudden shortness of breath, chest pain (especially worse with breathing), rapid heart rate, or coughing up blood are signs that require immediate emergency medical attention.
