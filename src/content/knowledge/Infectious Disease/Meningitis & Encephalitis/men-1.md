## Overview

Meningitis is inflammation of the membranes (meninges) surrounding the brain and spinal cord, most often caused by bacteria (including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Listeria), viruses (such as enteroviruses and HSV), fungi (such as Cryptococcus), or, less commonly, tuberculosis. Diagnosis typically involves a lumbar puncture with analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency treated with empiric antibiotics, often alongside dexamethasone to reduce inflammation, while other forms are managed according to their specific cause.

## Nutritional considerations

People with meningitis are often hospitalized, and acute illness — including headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness, or neck stiffness — can make eating and drinking difficult; clinical teams may use nutrition support, including tube feeding, when oral intake is inadequate. As alertness and appetite return, adequate protein and calorie intake support tissue repair and recovery. For tuberculosis meningitis, nutritional assessment and support are recognized as an important part of overall TB care, since malnutrition is common in people with TB and can affect treatment outcomes. Hydration is managed carefully in hospital under clinical supervision, since fluid balance can affect intracranial pressure.

## Safety note

Nutrition supports recovery from meningitis but does not treat the underlying infection. Bacterial and fungal meningitis are medical emergencies requiring prompt antibiotic or antifungal treatment; anyone with sudden fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, or confusion should seek immediate medical care.
