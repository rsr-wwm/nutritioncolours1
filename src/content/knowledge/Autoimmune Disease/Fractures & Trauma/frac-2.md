## Overview

Hip fractures (femoral neck, intertrochanteric, and periprosthetic breaks) are among the most serious fractures in older adults, frequently linked to underlying osteoporosis and falls. Treatment is surgical — hemiarthroplasty, total hip replacement, or internal fixation — decided by an orthopedic surgeon based on fracture pattern and patient factors. Nutrition's relevant contributions are in reducing underlying fracture risk and supporting recovery afterward.

---

## Nutritional Considerations

- **Bone density and osteoporosis risk**: Low bone mineral density is a major risk factor for hip fracture. Lifelong adequate calcium and vitamin D intake, per NIH Office of Dietary Supplements guidance, supports bone density, though established osteoporosis typically also requires medical treatment (e.g., bisphosphonates), which diet does not replace.
- **Protein and sarcopenia**: Muscle loss (sarcopenia) with age increases fall risk, and falls are the proximate cause of most hip fractures. Adequate protein intake, distributed across meals, combined with resistance exercise as tolerated, supports muscle mass and may help reduce fall risk.
- **Post-fracture recovery nutrition**: After hip fracture surgery, malnutrition is common in older adults and is associated with worse outcomes; adequate protein and calorie intake during rehabilitation supports wound healing and muscle recovery.
- **Vitamin D and fall risk**: Vitamin D also supports muscle function, and correcting deficiency is part of many fall-prevention strategies used in geriatric care.

---

## Safety & When to Seek Care

This is educational information, not medical advice. Hip fracture is an orthopedic emergency requiring prompt surgical evaluation. Osteoporosis screening and treatment decisions should be made with a physician; nutrition supports but does not replace medical fracture-risk management.
