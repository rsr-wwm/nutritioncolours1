## Overview

Fractures range from simple closed breaks to complex open (compound) fractures requiring surgical stabilization. Diagnosis, classification, and treatment — casting, fixation, or surgery — are the domain of orthopedic medicine. Bone healing itself progresses through a well-defined biological sequence (hematoma formation, soft callus, hard callus, remodeling) that typically unfolds over weeks to months and is influenced by nutritional status.

---

## Nutritional Considerations

- **Protein**: Adequate protein intake supports the collagen matrix and soft-tissue repair involved in every stage of fracture healing; low protein intake has been associated with slower healing and worse outcomes, particularly in older adults.
- **Calcium and vitamin D**: These are foundational to bone mineralization during healing. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements provides age-specific intake recommendations for both; deficiency in either can impair bone repair.
- **Vitamin C**: As a cofactor for collagen synthesis, vitamin C from fruits and vegetables supports the connective tissue framework that new bone is built on.
- **Adequate total calories**: Significant calorie restriction during recovery can slow healing, since bone repair is a metabolically demanding process; this is not the time for aggressive weight-loss dieting.
- **Smoking and alcohol**: Both are well-documented to impair fracture healing and are worth avoiding during recovery, independent of any specific nutrient.

---

## Safety & When to Seek Care

This is educational information, not medical advice. All fracture diagnosis, immobilization, and surgical decisions must be made by an orthopedic specialist. Signs of infection (fever, increasing redness, drainage) around a fracture site or cast require prompt medical attention.
