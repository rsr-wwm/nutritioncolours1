## Overview

Most low back pain is "mechanical" — related to muscle strain, disc degeneration, or joint stress rather than a specific serious disease. It is usually diagnosed clinically, without imaging, and managed with activity modification, physical therapy, and time. A minority of cases involve nerve root irritation (sciatica) or "red flag" features (such as cauda equina syndrome) that need urgent evaluation. Nutrition's role is indirect but real, mainly through weight and inflammation.

---

## Nutritional Considerations

- **Weight management**: Excess abdominal weight increases mechanical load on the lumbar spine and shifts posture in ways that can aggravate mechanical back pain; sustainable weight management is a reasonable supportive goal.
- **Anti-inflammatory eating pattern**: A diet rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, and omega-3 sources is a sensible general approach, consistent with broader musculoskeletal health recommendations, though it is not a specific treatment for back pain.
- **Bone health**: For older adults, adequate calcium and vitamin D support vertebral bone health and may be relevant if osteoporosis or vertebral compression is a contributing factor.
- **Staying active**: While not a nutrition point per se, current guidelines emphasize that prolonged bed rest is not helpful for mechanical back pain and that maintaining activity, alongside a healthy diet supporting energy and recovery, is preferred.

---

## Safety & When to Seek Care

This is educational information, not medical advice. Seek prompt medical evaluation for back pain accompanied by fever, unexplained weight loss, loss of bladder or bowel control, saddle numbness, or progressive leg weakness — these are red-flag symptoms requiring urgent care. Persistent or worsening back pain should be evaluated by a physician or physical therapist.
