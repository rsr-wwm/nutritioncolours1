## Overview

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis, caused by gradual breakdown of joint cartilage, most often affecting the knees, hips, and hands. It is diagnosed clinically and with imaging, and managed with a combination of physical therapy, weight management, medication, and — in advanced cases — surgery. Nutrition is one of the more evidence-supported non-surgical levers for OA, mainly through its effect on body weight and inflammation.

---

## Nutritional Considerations

- **Weight management**: Excess body weight is one of the strongest modifiable risk factors for knee OA specifically, because each pound of body weight translates into several pounds of added force across the knee joint during walking. Even modest, sustained weight loss is associated with meaningful pain reduction in clinical studies.
- **Anti-inflammatory eating pattern**: Diets emphasizing vegetables, fruit, whole grains, olive oil, and fatty fish (a Mediterranean-style pattern) are associated with lower inflammatory markers and are a reasonable general recommendation for OA, alongside standard care.
- **Glucosamine and chondroitin**: These are among the most commonly used supplements for OA; however, clinical trial evidence for pain relief is mixed and inconsistent across studies. Patients considering them should discuss realistic expectations with their physician rather than assume a guaranteed benefit.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids**: Some evidence supports a modest anti-inflammatory effect from fish oil; fatty fish, walnuts, and flaxseed are reasonable dietary sources.
- **Vitamin D**: Adequate vitamin D status supports overall musculoskeletal health, though evidence that supplementation slows OA progression specifically is limited.

---

## Safety & When to Seek Care

This is educational information, not medical advice or a substitute for physical therapy or medical management. Osteoarthritis diagnosis and treatment planning, including whether surgery is eventually needed, should be directed by an orthopedist or rheumatologist. Sudden joint swelling, redness, warmth, or fever warrants prompt medical evaluation to rule out infection or another cause.
