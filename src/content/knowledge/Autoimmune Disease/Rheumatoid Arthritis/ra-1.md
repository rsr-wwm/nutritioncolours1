## Overview

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the lining of the joints (the synovium), causing pain, swelling, and progressive joint damage. Diagnosis and monitoring rely on markers such as rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies, X-ray evidence of erosions, and composite activity scores like CDAI or DAS28. Conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) — methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, and leflunomide — remain the foundation of treatment. Nutrition does not replace DMARD therapy, but a well-chosen eating pattern can help manage inflammation, protect bone density, and support overall joint health alongside standard rheumatology care.

## Nutritional Considerations

Several nutrition-related strategies have reasonable evidence in RA:

- **Anti-inflammatory eating pattern:** A Mediterranean-style diet — rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, olive oil, and fatty fish — is the pattern most consistently associated with modestly reduced disease activity and improved physical function in people with RA.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids:** Fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel) or fish oil supplementation has been studied for its potential to reduce joint tenderness and NSAID use, though effects are modest and should be discussed with a rheumatologist before starting supplements.
- **Weight management:** Maintaining a healthy body weight reduces mechanical stress on weight-bearing joints and may improve how well biologic and DMARD therapies work.
- **Bone health:** RA itself, along with corticosteroid use, raises the risk of osteoporosis. Adequate dietary calcium and vitamin D support bone density; a clinician may recommend monitoring bone mineral density.
- **Methotrexate and folic acid:** Patients on methotrexate are typically prescribed folic acid supplementation by their rheumatologist to reduce side effects — this should be managed by the prescribing physician, not self-directed.
- **Limiting pro-inflammatory foods:** Reducing highly processed foods, excess red/processed meat, and added sugar is a reasonable, low-risk step, though no single food or elimination diet has been shown to reverse or cure RA.

## Safety Note

Dietary changes are a supportive complement to, not a substitute for, DMARD or biologic therapy prescribed by a rheumatologist. Anyone considering supplements (including fish oil or high-dose vitamins) should discuss this with their treating physician, particularly because of potential interactions with RA medications.
