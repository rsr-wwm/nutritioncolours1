## Overview

"Heart disease" is an umbrella term covering several distinct conditions: coronary artery disease (narrowed arteries supplying the heart), heart failure (reduced pumping or filling capacity), arrhythmias (abnormal heart rhythms), valvular disease (damaged heart valves), and cardiomyopathy (diseased heart muscle). Each has its own diagnosis and treatment pathway led by cardiology, but many share common risk factors — high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, obesity, diabetes, and smoking — and a common evidence base for dietary risk reduction.

## Nutritional & Dietary Considerations

Nutrition is one of the most well-supported, modifiable levers across the heart disease spectrum.

**The DASH and Mediterranean patterns**: Both are recommended by the American Heart Association and NIH/NHLBI as foundational eating patterns for cardiovascular risk reduction, emphasizing vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, and healthy oils.

**Sodium and blood pressure**: Reducing sodium intake, primarily from processed and restaurant foods, supports healthy blood pressure — a shared risk factor across coronary disease, heart failure, and stroke.

**Saturated fat and cholesterol**: Limiting saturated fat helps manage LDL cholesterol, a central driver of atherosclerosis underlying coronary artery disease and peripheral artery disease.

**Fiber and weight management**: Fiber-rich foods and a healthy body weight, achieved through diet and physical activity, support cholesterol, blood pressure, and blood sugar control simultaneously.

Because each heart disease category has its own specific dietary nuances — sodium and fluid restriction in heart failure, vitamin K consistency with certain anticoagulants in arrhythmias and valve disease — patients should follow the guidance specific to their diagnosis alongside general heart-healthy eating.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This information is educational and is intended to support, not replace, care from your cardiologist or physician. Blood pressure and cholesterol targets, medication decisions, and treatment plans must be individualized by your care team. Dietary changes work alongside — not instead of — prescribed cardiovascular medication and monitoring.
