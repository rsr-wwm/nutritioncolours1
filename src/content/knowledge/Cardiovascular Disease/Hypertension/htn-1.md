## Overview

High blood pressure (hypertension) is diagnosed when repeated readings show the force of blood against artery walls is consistently elevated. It is typically categorized into stages of increasing severity, with the highest stage (hypertensive crisis) requiring emergency care. Because hypertension usually has no symptoms, home blood pressure monitoring with a validated, properly sized cuff — taken at consistent times, seated and rested — is one of the most useful tools for tracking control between clinic visits. Causes range from lifestyle and genetic factors (essential hypertension) to identifiable secondary causes such as kidney or hormonal conditions.

## Nutritional & Dietary Considerations

Diet is one of the most well-evidenced tools for lowering blood pressure, alongside physical activity, weight management, and prescribed medication.

**The DASH eating pattern**: The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, developed and studied through NIH-funded research, emphasizes vegetables, fruits, whole grains, low-fat dairy, poultry, fish, beans, and nuts, while limiting red meat, sweets, and sugar-sweetened beverages. It remains one of the most consistently effective dietary patterns for lowering blood pressure.

**Sodium reduction**: Reducing sodium intake — largely by cutting back on processed, packaged, and restaurant foods rather than the salt shaker alone — is a cornerstone recommendation from the American Heart Association and NIH for blood pressure management.

**Potassium-rich foods**: Foods naturally rich in potassium, such as leafy greens, beans, and many fruits and vegetables, can help offset sodium's effects on blood pressure in people without kidney disease; patients with kidney impairment should discuss potassium intake with their clinician first.

**Alcohol and weight**: Limiting alcohol intake and achieving a healthy body weight are both independently associated with lower blood pressure and are part of standard lifestyle guidance from the American Heart Association.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This information is educational and is intended to support, not replace, care from your cardiologist or physician. Blood pressure and cholesterol targets, medication decisions, and treatment plans must be individualized by your care team. Dietary changes work alongside — not instead of — prescribed cardiovascular medication and monitoring.
