## Overview

ALS is diagnosed based on a combination of upper and lower motor neuron signs, disease progression, and exclusion of mimicking conditions, using frameworks such as the El Escorial criteria. Riluzole, taken orally, and edaravone, given intravenously or orally, are the two FDA-approved medications shown to modestly slow disease progression, though neither is curative. Tofersen is a newer, gene-targeted treatment approved specifically for the SOD1-mutation form of ALS. As respiratory muscles weaken, non-invasive ventilation is commonly introduced, and palliative care involvement supports quality of life throughout the disease course.

## Nutritional and Dietary Considerations

Nutrition is one of the most clinically significant, well-established components of ALS care. Hypermetabolism (elevated resting energy expenditure) combined with progressive dysphagia commonly leads to unintentional weight loss, which is independently associated with faster functional decline and shorter survival — making calorie and protein maintenance a genuine clinical priority, not just a comfort measure. As swallowing difficulty worsens, texture-modified diets and, eventually, PEG (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy) feeding tube placement are recommended to maintain adequate nutrition and reduce aspiration risk; ALS clinical guidelines generally favor considering a feeding tube proactively while respiratory function is still adequate, rather than waiting until swallowing has failed significantly. One practical medication-related note: riluzole is better absorbed on an empty stomach, and its label recommends taking it at a consistent time relative to meals — patients should follow their prescriber's specific instructions on timing rather than a generic rule.

## Safety Note

No diet slows the underlying neurodegeneration of ALS — riluzole and edaravone are disease-modifying treatments, and nutrition support is about maintaining weight, strength, and swallowing safety alongside them. Nutritional decisions, including feeding tube timing, should be made with the ALS multidisciplinary care team (neurologist, dietitian, speech-language pathologist). New choking episodes or rapid weight loss should be reported to the care team promptly.
