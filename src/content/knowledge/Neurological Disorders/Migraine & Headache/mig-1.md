## Overview

Migraine is a neurological condition causing recurrent, often one-sided headaches, sometimes preceded by aura (temporary visual, sensory, or speech disturbances). Common triggers include stress, hormonal fluctuations, poor sleep, and certain foods, though triggers vary significantly between individuals. Acute treatment options include triptans, NSAIDs, the newer gepants (ubrogepant, rimegepant), and anti-nausea medications for associated symptoms. Status migrainosus refers to a severe migraine attack lasting more than 72 hours, which may require medical treatment.

## Nutritional and Dietary Considerations

Two nutrients have real, guideline-recognized evidence for migraine prevention: magnesium and riboflavin (vitamin B2). Both are listed as possibly effective preventive options in headache society treatment guidelines, and are generally low-risk, making them reasonable to discuss with a healthcare provider as part of a broader prevention plan — though they are not a substitute for prescribed preventive therapy in patients with frequent or disabling migraine. Certain foods are commonly reported triggers, including alcohol (especially red wine), caffeine withdrawal, aged cheeses, and processed meats containing nitrates, though the evidence for specific food triggers is more individual and less consistent than often assumed; a headache diary tracking food, sleep, stress, and hormonal patterns is a practical way to identify personal triggers rather than following a generic elimination diet. Regular meal timing matters too, since skipping meals and the resulting drop in blood sugar is a recognized trigger for some people.

## Safety Note

Nutritional strategies are supportive and preventive, not a treatment for an acute migraine attack in progress. Magnesium and riboflavin supplementation should be discussed with a physician, particularly for people with kidney disease or those on other medications. Any new headache pattern, "worst headache of my life," or headache with neurological symptoms (weakness, confusion, vision loss) requires urgent medical evaluation.

When analyzing the cellular microenvironment, it is evident that targeted nutrient availability plays a crucial role in mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades. Deficiencies in these key areas often exacerbate the underlying mechanisms of mig 1, leading to an increased frequency of acute exacerbations and a general decline in the patient's quality of life. By focusing on nutrient density and optimal absorption rates, practitioners can build a robust defense against the systemic effects of the disease.
