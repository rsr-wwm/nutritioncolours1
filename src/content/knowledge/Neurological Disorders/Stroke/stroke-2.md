## Overview

Intracerebral hemorrhage (bleeding within the brain tissue itself) is most often caused by chronic hypertension or, in older adults, cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Subarachnoid hemorrhage, frequently from a ruptured aneurysm, involves bleeding into the space surrounding the brain and can also result from arteriovenous malformation (AVM) rupture. Both are neurological emergencies requiring rapid evaluation, blood pressure control, and often reversal of anticoagulation or surgical evacuation of the bleed. After subarachnoid hemorrhage, patients are monitored closely for vasospasm (delayed narrowing of brain blood vessels), typically managed with the medication nimodipine.

## Nutritional and Dietary Considerations

Since chronic high blood pressure is the leading cause of intracerebral hemorrhage, dietary sodium reduction and a DASH-style eating pattern are genuinely important, evidence-based components of long-term prevention once a patient has recovered from the acute event. During acute hospitalization, nutrition is managed clinically — often via feeding tube if the patient cannot swallow safely — and formal swallowing evaluation is standard before resuming oral intake, given the high rate of dysphagia after significant brain injury. There is no dietary intervention proven to affect vasospasm risk or outcomes after subarachnoid hemorrhage; that risk is managed medically. As recovery progresses, adequate protein and calorie intake support rehabilitation and wound/surgical healing.

## Safety Note

Hemorrhagic stroke is a medical emergency with no role for dietary self-management during the acute phase. Any resumption of oral intake after a significant brain bleed should be guided by a formal swallowing assessment. Blood pressure-focused dietary changes for long-term prevention should be planned with the medical team, especially given interactions with blood pressure medications.
