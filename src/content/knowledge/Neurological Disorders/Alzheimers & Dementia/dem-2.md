## Overview

Pharmacologic treatment for Alzheimer's disease falls into two broad categories. Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and the NMDA receptor antagonist memantine are symptomatic therapies that can modestly improve or stabilize cognitive and functional symptoms; they do not stop the underlying disease process. Newer anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, including lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab, target amyloid-beta plaques directly and are approved for early Alzheimer's disease, with modest effects on slowing decline. Non-pharmacological interventions — structured routines, cognitive engagement, and caregiver support — remain an essential part of comprehensive care.

## Nutritional and Dietary Considerations

Nutrition plays a supportive rather than therapeutic role alongside these medications. Cholinesterase inhibitors commonly cause gastrointestinal side effects such as nausea, reduced appetite, and weight loss, particularly during dose titration; taking these medications with food and monitoring weight can help manage this. Adequate hydration and consistent, nutrient-dense meals are especially important in older adults with dementia, who are at elevated risk of unintentional weight loss and malnutrition as the disease progresses. A Mediterranean- or MIND-style dietary pattern is reasonable as a general brain-healthy approach during treatment, though it has not been shown to enhance the effectiveness of these medications. Patients and caregivers should be aware that some dietary supplements can interact with prescription medications or affect absorption, so any supplement use should be reviewed with the prescribing clinician or pharmacist.

## Safety Note

None of these medications' effectiveness is proven to change based on diet, and no food or supplement should be used as a substitute for prescribed therapy. Anti-amyloid therapies in particular carry serious potential side effects (including brain swelling or bleeding) that require monitoring by a neurologist — nutrition has no role in managing these specific risks. Always coordinate any dietary or supplement changes with the treating physician.
