# Kidney Stones Nutrition

## Clinical Definition
Kidney stones (nephrolithiasis) are solid mineral and salt deposits that form within the kidney. Types include [calcium](/knowledge/nutrient-database/mineral-reference-data/calcium/min-ca-1) oxalate, calcium phosphate, uric acid, struvite, and cystine stones. They can cause flank pain, hematuria, and urinary obstruction. Management involves hydration, dietary modifications, and sometimes medical or surgical intervention.

## Nutrition
### What to Eat
- **High‑water foods** – cucumbers, watermelon, citrus fruits – help increase fluid intake.
- **Calcium‑rich foods** (dietary, not supplements) – low‑fat dairy, fortified plant milks – bind oxalate in the gut.
- **Low‑oxalate vegetables** – broccoli, cauliflower, kale, peas.
- **Citrate‑rich foods** – lemons, oranges, limes – inhibit stone formation.
- **Moderate‑protein plant sources** – lentils, beans – provide protein without excess purines.

### What to Avoid
- **High‑oxalate foods** – spinach, nuts, chocolate, beets, rhubarb.
- **Excessive animal protein** – red meat, poultry, fish – increases calcium and uric acid excretion.
- **High‑[sodium](/knowledge/nutrient-database/mineral-reference-data/sodium-chloride/min-na-1) foods** – processed snacks, canned soups – raises calcium excretion.
- **Sugar‑sweetened beverages** – soda, fruit drinks – increase calcium and oxalate excretion.

### Timing & Meal Planning
- **Hydration target**: Aim for ≥ 2.5 L of fluid daily (including water and high‑water foods).
- **Spread calcium intake**: Distribute dairy/fortified foods throughout the day to bind oxalate.
- **Limit oxalate with meals**: Pair oxalate‑rich foods with calcium sources to reduce absorption.

### Special Considerations
- **Stone type testing**: Tailor diet to stone composition (e.g., low‑purine for uric acid stones).
- **Kidney function**: If CKD coexist, adjust [potassium](/knowledge/nutrient-database/mineral-reference-data/potassium/min-k-1) and [phosphorus](/knowledge/nutrient-database/mineral-reference-data/phosphorus/min-p-1) as needed.
- **Vitamin C**: High doses can increase oxalate; limit to ≤ 500 mg/day.

## Pros & Cons Table
| Aspect | Medical Intervention | Nutritional Approach |
|--------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Pros** | Shock wave lithotripsy, ureteroscopy, or medication can remove or dissolve stones quickly. | Dietary changes can prevent recurrence, reduce need for invasive procedures, and improve overall health. |
| **Cons** | Procedures carry risks (infection, injury) and may require anesthesia. | Strict dietary restrictions can be challenging; inadequate calcium can paradoxically increase stone risk. |

## Frequently Asked Questions
<details><summary>What amount of water should I drink daily?</summary>
Aim for at least 2.5 L of fluids (≈ 8‑10 cups) spread throughout the day.
</details>

<details><summary>Can dairy increase stone risk?</summary>
Dietary calcium is protective; avoid excessive calcium supplements without medical advice.
</details>

<details><summary>Why avoid high‑oxalate foods?</summary>
Oxalate binds calcium in urine, forming calcium‑oxalate stones.
</details>

<details><summary>Is lemon juice helpful?</summary>
Citric acid from lemons increases urinary citrate, which inhibits stone formation.
</details>

<details><summary>How does salt affect stones?</summary>
High sodium increases calcium excretion, raising stone risk.
</details>

<details><summary>Do I need to limit protein?</summary>
Moderate animal protein (≈ 0.8 g/kg) is recommended; excess raises calcium and uric acid.
</details>

<details><summary>Can I eat nuts?</summary>
Limited amounts; choose low‑oxalate nuts like macadamia and avoid almonds.
</details>

<details><summary>What about tea?</summary>
Black tea contains oxalate; limit to ≤ 1 cup per day.
</details>

<details><summary>Are fruits safe?</summary>
Most fruits are fine; avoid high‑oxalate fruits like figs and kiwi in large amounts.
</details>

<details><summary>Should I take a calcium supplement?</summary>
Only if prescribed; excess supplemental calcium can raise stone risk.
</details>

<details><summary>How does vitamin D affect stones?</summary>
Adequate vitamin D supports calcium balance; excess may increase calcium absorption.
</details>

<details><summary>Can I still have coffee?</summary>
Moderate coffee is acceptable; avoid sweetened or high‑oxalate additives.
</details>

<details><summary>What is the role of [magnesium](/knowledge/nutrient-database/mineral-reference-data/magnesium/min-mg-1)?</summary>
Magnesium can bind oxalate; include magnesium‑rich foods like whole grains.
</details>

<details><summary>How often should I get urine tests?</summary>
Periodic 24‑hour urine analysis helps monitor stone risk factors.
</details>

<details><summary>Does obesity increase stone risk?</summary>
Higher body weight is associated with increased calcium and uric acid excretion.
</details>
