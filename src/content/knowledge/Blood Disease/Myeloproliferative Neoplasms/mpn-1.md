## Overview

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are a group of blood cancers in which the bone marrow overproduces one or more types of blood cells. Polycythemia vera causes an excess of red blood cells; essential thrombocythemia causes excess platelets; and myelofibrosis involves progressive scarring (fibrosis) of the bone marrow, which can lead to anemia and an enlarged spleen. Many cases are associated with a mutation in the JAK2 gene. Because these conditions thicken the blood or alter platelet function, the main clinical concern is an increased risk of blood clots (thrombosis) and, in some cases, bleeding. Treatments include phlebotomy (removing blood to lower red cell counts), low-dose aspirin, cytoreductive medications (such as hydroxyurea), and JAK inhibitors (such as ruxolitinib) for myelofibrosis.

## Nutritional Considerations

- **Cardiovascular-supportive eating**: Since thrombosis is the primary complication across MPNs, a heart-healthy dietary pattern — emphasizing vegetables, fruit, whole grains, lean protein, and limited sodium and saturated fat — is a reasonable supportive strategy, similar to general cardiovascular risk reduction.
- **Hydration**: Adequate fluid intake is generally recommended, particularly around phlebotomy procedures.
- **Iron and phlebotomy**: Phlebotomy for polycythemia vera intentionally lowers iron stores as part of treatment; iron supplements should not be taken to "correct" this unless a hematologist specifically identifies symptomatic iron deficiency, since replacing iron can counteract the treatment.
- **Supplement caution**: Because low-dose aspirin is often part of treatment, other supplements with blood-thinning properties (high-dose fish oil, vitamin E, ginkgo) should be used only with a hematologist's input to avoid additive bleeding risk.
- **Weight and activity**: Maintaining a healthy weight and staying physically active (as tolerated) support general vascular health, which is relevant given the clotting risk associated with these conditions.

## Safety & When to See a Doctor

Report symptoms of a possible blood clot — leg swelling or pain, chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, or vision changes — to a doctor immediately, as these can be medical emergencies. MPNs require ongoing monitoring by a hematologist; this content is educational and does not replace individualized treatment.
