## Overview

Hemophilia A and B are inherited, X-linked bleeding disorders caused by a deficiency of clotting factor VIII (hemophilia A) or factor IX (hemophilia B, also called Christmas disease). Because clotting factor levels are low, people with hemophilia bleed longer than normal after injury and can experience spontaneous bleeding into joints and muscles, which over time can cause joint damage. Modern treatment includes factor replacement therapy (plasma-derived or recombinant), non-factor therapies such as emicizumab, and, more recently, gene therapy for eligible patients.

## Nutritional Considerations

Diet does not correct a clotting factor deficiency, but nutrition and lifestyle choices affect bleeding risk and joint health:

- **Weight management**: Excess body weight increases mechanical stress on joints that are already vulnerable to bleeding-related damage (hemarthrosis), so maintaining a healthy weight is commonly recommended.
- **Bone and joint support**: Adequate calcium and vitamin D intake support bone health, which is particularly relevant if joint disease limits weight-bearing physical activity.
- **Medications that increase bleeding risk**: Aspirin and other NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) impair platelet function and are generally avoided for pain relief; acetaminophen is typically preferred, but any medication choice should be confirmed with a hematology team.
- **Alcohol**: Heavy alcohol use can impair liver function and increase bleeding risk, so it is usually advised to limit or avoid it.

## Safety & When to See a Doctor

Hemophilia management should always be directed by a hematologist or a specialized hemophilia treatment center. Seek urgent care for joint swelling and pain suggesting a bleed, head injury of any kind, or unusual or heavy bleeding.
