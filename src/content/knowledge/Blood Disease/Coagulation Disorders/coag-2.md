## Overview

Von Willebrand disease (vWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder, caused by a deficiency or dysfunction of von Willebrand factor, a protein needed for platelets to stick together and to the vessel wall at an injury site. It ranges from mild (Type 1) to more severe forms (Type 2 subtypes, and the rare Type 3). Platelet disorders are a separate but related group: immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an autoimmune condition where the immune system destroys platelets; thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare, life-threatening disorder involving small blood clots that consume platelets; and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an adverse immune reaction to the blood thinner heparin. Treatments differ substantially by condition — from desmopressin (DDAVP) and von Willebrand factor concentrates for vWD, to immune-modulating therapies for ITP, to emergency plasma exchange for TTP.

## Nutritional Considerations

- **Medications to avoid**: Aspirin and other NSAIDs impair platelet function and can worsen bleeding symptoms in vWD and platelet disorders; they are generally avoided unless a hematologist advises otherwise.
- **Alcohol**: Alcohol can suppress platelet production and impair platelet function, so limiting intake is commonly advised for people with a bleeding tendency.
- **Supplements**: High-dose fish oil, vitamin E, and certain herbal supplements (such as ginkgo) have mild blood-thinning effects and should be discussed with a hematologist before use.
- **General nutrition**: There is no specific diet that treats vWD or these platelet disorders; the role of nutrition is largely supportive — maintaining overall health and, for TTP or HIT specifically, supporting recovery during and after acute hospital-based treatment.

## Safety & When to See a Doctor

TTP and severe HIT are medical emergencies. Seek immediate medical care for unexplained widespread bruising, unusual bleeding, confusion, severe headache, or neurological symptoms, especially if you have started a new medication (including heparin) or have a known platelet disorder. Bleeding disorder management should always be guided by a hematologist.
