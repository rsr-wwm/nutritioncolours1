## Overview

Ascites is fluid buildup in the abdominal cavity driven by portal hypertension and low albumin from advanced liver disease. It's diagnosed with diagnostic paracentesis and the serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG). Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is an infection of that ascitic fluid without an obvious surgical source, treated with antibiotics such as ceftriaxone. Management combines sodium restriction and diuretics (typically spironolactone plus furosemide), with large-volume paracentesis and albumin infusion for tense or refractory ascites, and TIPS considered for select refractory cases.

## Nutritional & dietary considerations

- **Sodium restriction is a cornerstone of ascites management**, but the specific target should be set by the treating clinician based on individual severity and response — there isn't one number that applies to everyone.
- **Fluid restriction is generally reserved for significant hyponatremia (low blood sodium)**, not applied routinely to everyone with ascites; unnecessary fluid restriction can cause its own problems.
- **Adequate protein and calorie intake remains important** even while restricting sodium — malnutrition is common in this population and undermining nutrition to chase a stricter sodium number is counterproductive.
- **Avoid NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, naproxen, etc.), which can worsen kidney function and fluid retention in cirrhosis with ascites.
- **Alcohol should be avoided entirely.**

## Safety note

Sodium and fluid targets in ascites must be individualized and adjusted over time by a hepatology team — self-directed severe restriction can cause poor nutrition intake and worse outcomes. SBP is a medical emergency requiring prompt antibiotic treatment, not a condition managed through diet.
