## Overview

Cirrhosis is the end stage of chronic liver injury, in which fibrosis and regenerative nodules replace healthy liver architecture. It can arise from viral hepatitis, alcohol, MASLD/NASH, autoimmune disease, and other causes. Compensated cirrhosis has no major complications yet; decompensated cirrhosis involves ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice. The Child-Pugh score (A–C) and MELD-Na score are used to estimate prognosis and prioritize patients for liver transplant.

## Nutritional & dietary considerations

- **Malnutrition and sarcopenia (muscle loss) are extremely common in cirrhosis** and are independently associated with worse outcomes — this makes adequate nutrition, not restriction, the priority.
- **Protein adequacy matters.** Current hepatology guidance recommends against routine protein restriction, including in patients with encephalopathy; protein needs are typically higher than in healthy adults due to altered metabolism, and should be set individually by a clinician or dietitian rather than a fixed number applied to everyone.
- **Frequent, smaller meals with a late-evening snack** help prevent the overnight muscle breakdown that people with cirrhosis are prone to, since liver glycogen stores are reduced.
- **Sodium restriction is often recommended once ascites develops** (see the Ascites page in this pillar), and should be set by the treating team based on individual fluid status.
- **Alcohol should be avoided entirely** regardless of the underlying cause of cirrhosis.

## Safety note

Nutrition needs in cirrhosis vary significantly by Child-Pugh/MELD stage, presence of ascites or encephalopathy, and individual lab values. This is not a condition to self-manage with a generic diet — work with a hepatologist and registered dietitian to set specific protein, sodium, and calorie targets.
