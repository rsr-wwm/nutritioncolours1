## Overview

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a reversible neuropsychiatric complication of advanced liver disease, caused by the buildup of ammonia and other gut-derived toxins that a failing liver can't clear, combined with portosystemic shunting that bypasses the liver entirely. Severity is graded using the West Haven criteria (grade 0–IV, from subtle cognitive changes to coma). First-line treatment is lactulose, which acidifies the colon and increases ammonia excretion in stool; rifaximin is often added to reduce ammonia-producing gut bacteria. Common triggers include infection, constipation, GI bleeding, dehydration, and sedative medications.

## Nutritional & dietary considerations

- **Protein restriction is an outdated myth.** Current AASLD/EASL guidance recommends against routinely restricting protein in HE — adequate protein intake supports muscle mass (low muscle mass itself worsens ammonia handling) and does not worsen most patients' encephalopathy.
- **Plant-based and dairy protein sources are sometimes better tolerated** than large amounts of red meat in some patients, though this varies individually and isn't a universal rule.
- **Preventing constipation** through adequate fiber and fluid (as tolerated and cleared by your care team) supports ammonia clearance, since lactulose's laxative effect is part of how it works.
- **A late-evening snack** helps offset the reduced glycogen reserves common in cirrhosis and reduces overnight muscle breakdown.
- **Sedatives and alcohol should be avoided**, since both can precipitate or worsen an HE episode.

## Safety note

Specific protein and fluid targets for a person with HE must be individualized by their hepatology team based on nutritional status, kidney function, and episode severity — this is not a condition to manage with a fixed home diet. A sudden change in confusion, mood, or alertness in someone with cirrhosis warrants prompt medical evaluation.
