## Overview

Esophageal varices are enlarged, fragile veins that form in the esophagus when portal hypertension from cirrhosis reroutes blood flow. They're identified through screening endoscopy in at-risk patients. Bleeding prevention relies on non-selective beta-blockers (such as propranolol or carvedilol) and/or endoscopic variceal band ligation. An acute variceal bleed is a medical emergency treated with vasoactive drugs (octreotide, terlipressin), endoscopic therapy, and occasionally balloon tamponade or TIPS for bleeding that doesn't respond to initial treatment.

## Nutritional & dietary considerations

- **Diet does not prevent or treat variceal bleeding directly** — beta-blockers and endoscopic therapy are the evidence-based interventions.
- **After band ligation**, clinicians typically advise a short period of soft, non-irritating foods and avoiding very hot, hard, or sharp foods while the treated area heals; follow your endoscopist's specific post-procedure instructions.
- **Avoid NSAIDs and aspirin** unless specifically directed by your care team, since they increase bleeding risk.
- **Alcohol should be avoided entirely**, as it worsens portal hypertension and liver function.
- **Overall nutritional status matters for procedural safety and recovery** — malnutrition increases risk around bleeding episodes and procedures, so maintaining adequate protein and calorie intake (as part of the broader cirrhosis nutrition plan) is worthwhile between episodes.

## Safety note

Sudden vomiting of blood or black, tarry stools are signs of a possible variceal bleed and require emergency care immediately — this is not a situation to manage at home or wait out. Nutrition guidance around procedures should come directly from the treating gastroenterology/hepatology team.
