## Overview

Alcohol withdrawal ranges from mild symptoms tracked with tools like the CIWA-Ar score to life-threatening delirium tremens, and is typically managed with a supervised benzodiazepine taper in a medical setting. Wernicke encephalopathy is an acute thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency emergency — causing confusion, unsteady gait, and abnormal eye movements — that is common in people with alcohol use disorder due to poor intake and impaired thiamine absorption/storage. Left untreated, it can progress to irreversible Korsakoff psychosis with severe memory impairment.

## Nutritional & dietary considerations

- **Thiamine repletion is the critical nutrition-related intervention**, but it must be given by a clinician — often intravenously and, importantly, before or alongside any glucose administration, since giving glucose first can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy in a thiamine-deficient person. This is not something to attempt with over-the-counter supplements at home.
- **Broader micronutrient deficiencies are common** in alcohol use disorder, including folate, vitamin B6, and magnesium, and benefit from clinical evaluation and lab-guided repletion rather than guesswork.
- **Nutritional rehabilitation is one part of a larger medical and psychosocial recovery plan**, alongside supervised withdrawal management and, often, longer-term treatment for alcohol use disorder.
- **Once medically stabilized**, a general nutrient-dense diet supports ongoing recovery, but it does not substitute for the urgent thiamine treatment needed in acute Wernicke encephalopathy.

## Safety note

Suspected Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, poor coordination, abnormal eye movements in someone with heavy alcohol use) is a medical emergency requiring immediate treatment — delays can cause permanent brain damage. Alcohol withdrawal itself can also be medically dangerous and should be managed under supervision, not alone.
