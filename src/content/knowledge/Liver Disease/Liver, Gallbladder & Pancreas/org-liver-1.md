## Overview

The liver is the body's largest internal organ and sits at the crossroads of digestion and metabolism. Structurally, it is organized into lobes and microscopic units called lobules, each built around a central vein and supplied by a "portal triad" of a small hepatic artery branch, portal vein branch, and bile duct. Functionally, the liver synthesizes proteins (including albumin and clotting factors), manages carbohydrate metabolism (storing and releasing glucose), processes fats and cholesterol, detoxifies drugs and other compounds in two enzymatic phases, and produces bile to help digest dietary fat. Blood from the intestines passes through the liver via the portal vein before reaching the rest of the body — a "first-pass" arrangement that makes the liver central to nutrient processing. Notably, the liver has a strong capacity to regenerate: it can regrow much of its mass even after significant surgical resection or injury, given adequate time and a healthy underlying state.

## Why This Matters for Nutrition

Every major nutrient class you eat is processed by the liver — carbohydrates for blood sugar regulation, proteins for building blood proteins and enzymes, and fats for cholesterol handling and bile-dependent absorption. Adequate, balanced nutrition supports the liver's regenerative capacity and day-to-day workload, while excess alcohol, chronically high sugar or saturated fat intake, and certain medications can add metabolic strain. This is why diet quality is a cornerstone of both preventing and managing liver disease, and why a registered dietitian is often part of the care team for anyone with abnormal liver function.

## Safety Note

This is a general educational overview. Anyone with abnormal liver function tests, a diagnosed liver condition, or symptoms such as jaundice or unexplained fatigue should consult a physician, typically a hepatologist or gastroenterologist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1: Can the liver really regenerate itself?
**A1:** Yes — the liver has a notable capacity to regrow functional tissue after injury or partial surgical removal, which is part of why living-donor liver transplantation is possible. Regeneration works best when the underlying liver is otherwise healthy and well-nourished.

### Q2: How does nutrition affect liver detoxification?
**A2:** The liver's detoxification enzymes rely on adequate protein and specific micronutrients (B vitamins, sulfur-containing amino acids, antioxidants) to function properly. A balanced diet supports these pathways; there is no evidence that special "detox" products improve on this.

### Q3: Who should evaluate ongoing liver concerns?
**A3:** A physician — typically a hepatologist or gastroenterologist — should evaluate persistent liver test abnormalities or symptoms. A registered dietitian can then help build a supportive eating plan.
