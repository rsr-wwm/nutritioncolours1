## Overview

Soy milk is made by soaking, grinding, and boiling soybeans, then filtering out the solids (the leftover pulp, okara, is a byproduct). It has been consumed in East Asia for roughly two thousand years, well predating the modern plant-milk category, and is a byproduct-adjacent process to tofu-making.

## Nutritional profile

Soy milk is the highest-protein plant milk on the market, typically providing 7–9 g of protein per cup — comparable to cow's milk. Unlike most other plant proteins, soy protein contains all nine essential amino acids in proportions that support a high protein quality score, which is why it is often described as a "complete" plant protein. Most commercial soy milk is fortified with calcium, vitamin D, and B12.

## Isoflavones

Soybeans naturally contain isoflavones (genistein and daidzein), plant compounds that weakly bind estrogen receptors. In 1999 the U.S. FDA authorized a qualified health claim linking soy protein to reduced heart disease risk, based on its cholesterol-lowering effect; more recent evidence reviews have been more mixed, and the FDA proposed revisiting that claim in 2017. Isoflavone research on breast cancer risk has also produced mixed results across observational and clinical studies — current evidence does not support avoiding moderate soy intake, but individuals with a personal history of hormone-sensitive cancer should discuss soy intake with their oncologist.

## Safety notes

- **Soy allergy** is one of the more common food allergies and requires strict avoidance.
- **Thyroid function:** Isoflavones can interfere with iodine uptake in some studies, but this effect is generally not clinically significant except in people with pre-existing iodine deficiency or subclinical hypothyroidism — cooking/processing (as in commercial soy milk) reduces goitrogenic compounds further.
