## Overview

Raw milk cheese is made from milk that has not been pasteurized, so it retains a much broader native microbial population than pasteurized milk. Advocates describe this as preserving "terroir" — the idea that soil, pasture, breed, season, and local microbes give a cheese a distinct sense of place. Classic examples of traditionally raw-milk cheeses include Parmigiano-Reggiano, Comté, Gruyère, and Roquefort, though many producers now also make pasteurized versions of these styles.

## Regulation

In the United States, the FDA requires cheese made from unpasteurized milk to be aged for a minimum of 60 days at a temperature not less than 35°F (1.7°C) before sale. The rationale is that the combination of time, acidity, salt, and competition from the cheese's own starter cultures reduces pathogen levels during aging. The European Union permits a wider range of raw milk cheeses, including many short-aged and fresh varieties, under its own hygiene and traceability rules. Regulations vary considerably by country.

## Safety considerations

Raw milk cheeses carry a higher documented risk of pathogens — including *Listeria monocytogenes*, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, and, rarely, *Brucella* — than pasteurized-milk cheeses, even after the 60-day aging period. Public health agencies including the CDC and FDA advise pregnant people, young children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals to avoid raw milk cheeses altogether, and to choose pasteurized versions of these cheese styles instead.

## Nutritional profile

Raw and pasteurized versions of the same cheese style are broadly similar in macronutrient content (protein, fat, calcium). Any nutritional differences attributed to raw milk are modest compared with the food-safety trade-off, which is why the safety guidance above takes priority for higher-risk groups.
