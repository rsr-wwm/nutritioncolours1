## Overview

Yogurt is milk fermented by two specific bacterial species working symbiotically: *Streptococcus thermophilus* and *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus*. Regulatory standards in most countries require both live cultures to be present for a product to be labeled "yogurt." Many commercial yogurts add further probiotic strains (such as *L. acidophilus*, *L. casei*, or *Bifidobacterium lactis*) beyond these two.

## Nutritional profile

Plain yogurt is a good source of protein, calcium, and B vitamins (particularly B12 and riboflavin). Greek and Icelandic-style yogurts are strained to remove whey, roughly doubling protein density (typically 15–20 g per cup) compared with unstrained yogurt. The fermentation process also breaks down some lactose, which is why many people with mild lactose intolerance tolerate yogurt better than milk.

## Health evidence

Regular yogurt consumption is associated with gut and metabolic benefits in a substantial body of observational and clinical research. In March 2024, the U.S. FDA authorized a qualified health claim allowing yogurt labels to state that eating yogurt regularly (about 3 servings/week) may reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes — a qualified claim, meaning the FDA judges the evidence to be limited, not conclusive. Live-culture yogurt is also one of the better-studied probiotic foods for general digestive comfort. These benefits are most reliably associated with plain, unsweetened yogurt — heavily sweetened flavored varieties can offset the metabolic benefit with added sugar.

## Choosing yogurt

Look for a "Live & Active Cultures" label or an ingredient list naming specific bacterial strains, and check the sugar content on flavored varieties.
